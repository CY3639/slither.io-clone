Executive summary

This project turns Loonride's single-player Phaser Slither.io tutorial into a cloud-native multiplayer game, deployed on AWS in ap-southeast-2 as a vehicle for demonstrating scalable cloud architecture. The original sprites, renderer and gameplay rules are reused, but every outcome (movement, eating, growth, collision, death, score) is now decided by a server-side simulation. Browsers send only steering intent over WebSockets and render interpolated snapshots.

The unit of scale is an independent authoritative world: one ECS Fargate task runs one 50-snake world for up to four humans, with NPCs filling the remaining slots. Worlds share no real-time state, so capacity grows linearly by adding tasks (worlds = ceil(humans / 4)), while everything of lasting value leaves the task as events (SQS → Lambda → DynamoDB). CloudFront is the single HTTPS/WSS entry point: it serves the fingerprinted client from a private S3 bucket, caches the leaderboard for 10 s, and forwards WebSockets to an Application Load Balancer that accepts only CloudFront's secret header.

Key measured results:

  • ECS Service Auto Scaling took the service from 1 to 5 tasks in seven minutes (12:10–12:17 AEST) under 14 synthetic players, seating all of them across four worlds plus one empty headroom world, with 0 connection errors and RTT p50 16 ms. Measurement showed a full world uses only 20–30 % of its 0.25 vCPU, so a custom world-occupancy metric (target 75 %) is the primary scaling signal, with CPU as a safety net. ECS task scale-in protection prevents scale-in from ending games in progress. ⟦SCALE_IN_SUMMARY⟧
  • Edge caching: fingerprinted assets are CloudFront hits from the Brisbane edge (≈20 ms). The leaderboard API drops from 615 ms (miss) to ≈20 ms (hit).
  • Asynchronous, idempotent persistence: real game events flowed through SQS and Lambda into DynamoDB, including out-of-order delivery. A poison message was retried three times, dead-lettered, and raised an alarm.
  • Resilience: stopping a task that hosted four players moved them to other worlds within one 5 s sample. ECS replaced the task in under a minute, and the dying task flushed all 206 of its events.
  • Cost: measurement identified egress, not compute, as the main cost. Delta encoding and permessage-deflate cut bytes per player by ~85 %, giving ≈ US$0.011 per player-hour (≈ $495/month at 60 concurrent players).

The CAB432 account's guardrails (no IAM role, security group or certificate creation; no deletion of buckets or log groups) forced several adaptations, which are documented with their residual risks in §3.2.

1 Introduction

Real-time multiplayer games are a demanding workload for cloud architecture. They need stateful compute that updates many entities dozens of times per second, low-latency bidirectional communication, durable records of outcomes, and capacity that follows a player population that changes through the day. This project uses a small Slither.io-style game as a vehicle to build and measure such a system on AWS.

The starting point was Loonride's open-source tutorial clone of Slither.io (Loonride, 2017). It is a single-browser Phaser CE 2.6.2 game in which a P2 physics engine running in the page decides movement, collisions, food consumption and growth for one player and two bots. Nothing in it is networked, and the browser is the authority on every outcome, so a multiplayer version could not trust it. The project keeps the original sprites, renderer, movement constants and rules, and moves all authority to a server-side simulation.

Scope

  • Players enter an anonymous display name and join a world: an arena that always holds 50 snakes. At most 4 are humans and the rest are server-controlled NPCs (NPCs = 50 − humans).
  • The first human starts a lobby of at most 10 seconds. The world starts early when four humans are present. Later humans replace an NPC immediately. A fifth human receives ROOM_FULL and is routed elsewhere.
  • The server simulates movement, eating, growth, collisions and death at 30 Hz. Clients only send intent (heading and boost) and render interpolated snapshots.
  • Deaths and session results are stored durably and feed a global leaderboard.

Objectives

The engineering objectives were a cloud architecture that (i) scales horizontally with demand using non-Lambda auto-scaling, (ii) distributes connections across stateful workers, (iii) keeps durable data outside those workers, (iv) caches static content at the edge, (v) processes outcomes asynchronously and idempotently, (vi) is observable and recovers from failures, and (vii) stays cost-proportionate. Every claim in this report is backed by a captured artefact. Evidence identifiers such as [E11] refer to the register in Appendix B and files in the repository's evidence/ folder.

2.2 Independent authoritative worlds

The central decision is that the unit of scale is an independent world. A world is small (50 snakes, ≤4 humans), lives entirely in one worker's memory, and shares no real-time state with any other world. Scaling out means adding worlds, not partitioning one world across machines.

This removes distributed consistency from the hot path. Each 30 Hz tick is a single-threaded loop over local memory (measured at about 0.3 ms per tick for a full world, §4.5), with no cross-node locks, replicated physics or consensus protocol. It also makes capacity planning arithmetic: because NPCs are workload rather than users,

requiredWorlds = ceil(concurrentHumans / 4)        runningWorkers ≈ ceil(concurrentHumans / (4 × targetOccupancy))

The trade-off is explicit. A world's transient state exists only inside its worker. If that worker fails or is replaced, the positions and current lengths in that world are lost, and its players reconnect to another world within seconds. That is acceptable for a casual arena game, where a round is short and losing it costs a player one life. Everything with long-term value (scores, deaths, best scores, world summaries) leaves the worker as an event at the moment it happens and is stored durably. Restoring exact world state would need checkpointing or replication at tick rate, a large cost in complexity, bandwidth and latency for little player benefit. A single global world was rejected for the same reason: it would need spatial partitioning with entity hand-over and cross-node collision detection.

2.3 Data and control flows

  1. Join. Browser → CDN → load balancer → some worker. If the world has a free human slot, the player joins. Otherwise the worker replies ROOM_FULL and closes. The client retries after 150–600 ms jitter, so the load balancer can choose another worker. After five quick attempts it backs off exponentially (≤5 s) for about three minutes while the autoscaler adds a world.
  2. Lobby and start. The first human starts a ≤10 s countdown. The fourth human starts the world immediately. At start, empty slots are filled with NPCs.
  3. Play. Clients send intent at ≤20 Hz. The worker ticks at 30 Hz and sends each client a 15 Hz snapshot limited to its area of interest. Clients render 110 ms behind real time and interpolate.
  4. Persist. Outcomes become events with unique IDs → queue → worker function → conditional, transactional writes → ranked index → read API → edge cache.
  5. Scale. Workers publish occupancy and tick metrics. The autoscaler tracks targets for occupancy and CPU. Workers with players protect themselves from scale-in.

3.3 Security

  • Public vs private access. Only CloudFront is a public entry point. S3 is OAC-only (direct URL → 403). The ALB answers 403 without the origin secret. Tasks have no public IP [E2, E19].
  • Transport. HTTPS/WSS between player and edge, with HTTP redirected to HTTPS. The internal hop is discussed in §3.2.
  • Untrusted clients. The server accepts only four message types (join, in, respawn, ping), validates every field (finite numbers, ranges, UUID/hex formats), caps message size at 1 KB, sanitises and truncates display names to 16 characters (rendered with textContent), rate-limits each socket with a token bucket (40 msg/s, sustained abuse closes the socket), and ignores any position, score or length a client sends. Unit and integration tests cover forged fields and malformed JSON.
  • Identity without accounts. Anonymous player IDs are server-generated UUIDs signed with an HMAC key held in Secrets Manager. A client presenting an ID with a wrong signature gets a fresh ID, so leaderboard entries can't be hijacked (test: "forged id is replaced").
  • Credentials. No access keys exist anywhere: tasks and functions use IAM roles, and deploy scripts use the operator's SSO session.

3.5 Infrastructure as code and observability

Four CloudFormation stacks (data, backend, frontend, monitoring) plus idempotent CLI scripts reproduce the deployment with one command (infra/scripts/deploy.sh). All parameters come from environment variables, and every taggable resource carries purpose = assessment 3 and qut-username = n5528712@qut.edu.au. The data stack uses DeletionPolicy: Retain and is separate from compute, so the backend can be torn down without losing results. (The DynamoDB table survived an early failed stack and was imported into the new stack rather than deleted.)

Game tasks emit one JSON log line per event with taskId, worldId, playerId and eventId, so a single event can be followed from event_published in the game log to event_processed in the worker log. Custom metrics use the Embedded Metric Format: a JSON line every 10 s that CloudWatch Logs converts into metrics, so no metrics API permission or SDK is needed. The metric dimension is the service name only, so the Average across tasks is the fleet-wide value the scaling policy consumes.

4 Application implementation

4.1 From a single-player tutorial to an authoritative server

The original game delegated motion to P2 physics: the head was moved with moveForward, its positions were pushed into a headPath, each of 30 body sections was placed on that path about 17 × scale behind the previous one, and an invisible "edge" circle locked in front of the head killed the snake when it touched another snake's body. P2 is coupled to Phaser sprites and cannot run headless. The server therefore re-implements the same model directly (server/src/game/snake.js): the same constants (start scale 0.6, 30 sections, 130/200 px/s, a turn rate of 2 rad/s derived from P2's rotateLeft(40) and its px→m factor of 20, growth ×1.01 per food), arc-length placement of sections along the head path, and an edge-point collision test. A spatial hash grid makes collision and food queries local. A full 50-snake world ticks in about 0.3 ms on a laptop core and the 37 server tests pin the behaviour down.

The NPCs keep the original bot's "trend" wander (keep turning one way, flip with probability 1/20) and add three priorities: stay inside the arena, avoid bodies and approaching heads, and seek nearby food. Instrumenting deaths during development showed that 69 % of NPC deaths were head-to-head collisions while chasing the same food. Adding an "approaching head" check raised median NPC lifetime from 15 s to 23 s, and that was judged sufficient (the brief asks not to over-engineer AI).

The client keeps the original assets and drawing code (tinted circle sections, the shadow.js light-up effect while boosting, and the eye pair) but now only renders server state. Because circle.png is solid red, tinting could only produce reds. The client desaturates it once at start-up into a white texture, which keeps the original outline shading and lets each snake take any colour.

4.2 Workflows

Each task's Room runs a small state machine: idle → (first human) → lobby → (4 humans or 10 s) → running → (last human leaves) → idle. Leaving running discards the world entirely, so an empty task does no simulation work (≈3 % CPU, [E10]). Every tick enforces the slot invariant NPCs = 50 − humans: a late joiner removes the smallest NPC, and a departing human is replaced by a new NPC. A dead human keeps their slot during the 3 s respawn timer, so the active count briefly reads 49. Unit tests check the fill for 1–4 humans (49/48/47/46 NPCs), the ≤10 s lobby, the immediate start at four humans, ROOM_FULL for the fifth, late join, and world completion [E4, E5].

4.3 Networking, interest management and bandwidth

Snapshots contain only the snakes whose bounding box intersects the player's viewport plus a 350 px margin. Each snake is serialised once per snapshot and the fragment reused for every client. Food changes travel as deltas. The client buffers snapshots, maps server time to local time using the minimum observed one-way offset, and renders 110 ms in the past, interpolating section positions between the two surrounding snapshots.

Bandwidth was measured, not assumed, and it turned out to be the dominant running cost (§5). Two optimisations followed from the measurements, both captured by the load tester:

Version (same 4-player, 40 s run, 0.25 vCPU container)Bytes on the wirePer playerEvidence

Compression added about +2.5 points of host-core CPU (≈ +10 points of a 0.25 vCPU task), a good trade because egress costs more than compute in this design. Enabling compression also exposed a shutdown bug: sends become asynchronous, and the server terminated sockets before the final bye message was flushed. The integration test caught it, and shutdown now waits up to 1.5 s for close handshakes.

4.4 Asynchronous, idempotent persistence

The game loop never waits for the network to persist results. publish() appends an event envelope (eventId UUID, type, version, time, worldId, taskId, payload) to an in-memory buffer. A flusher sends batches of up to 10 with SendMessageBatch. Rejected entries are retried with exponential backoff and jitter, up to 4 times, and the buffer is bounded at 5,000 events (oldest dropped). An event that still can't be sent is written in full to the log (event_dropped), so it is recoverable. On SIGTERM the buffer is flushed within a deadline. In the resilience test the terminating task flushed 206 events with 0 dropped [E16].

SQS standard queues deliver at least once and do not preserve order, so the worker is written for both:

  • Idempotency. Non-idempotent changes (incrementing deaths and kills, inserting a death record) run in one TransactWriteItems together with a conditional put of EVENT#<eventId>. A redelivery fails the condition and changes nothing. The best-score update is a conditional "max" (attribute_not_exists(bestScore) OR bestScore < :s), which is idempotent by nature, so it runs before the transaction: a crash between the two can never lose a high score.
  • Ordering. World records use SET updates, so WORLD_STARTED and WORLD_COMPLETED commute. In the deployment a PLAYER_DIED was processed before its world's WORLD_STARTED [E8], and the result was still correct.
  • Poison messages. Schema validation failures and transient errors are both reported as batchItemFailures, so only those records return to the queue. After three receives SQS moves them to the DLQ, which raises an alarm. A deliberately malformed message was rejected three times, 60 s apart (the visibility timeout), then moved to the DLQ, and the dlq-not-empty alarm went to ALARM [E16].

The leaderboard is read by a separate Lambda behind the ALB. It queries the sparse GSI in descending order and returns Cache-Control: public, max-age=10. CloudFront therefore answers repeated reads at the edge (§4.7), and at most one request per 10 s per edge location reaches DynamoDB, regardless of player count.

4.5 Scaling: design and measured behaviour

Choosing the signal from measurements

The brief suggested starting with CPU target tracking. Local measurement in a container capped at 0.25 vCPU, followed by Fargate measurement, showed why CPU alone can't drive this system. A world can never hold more than four humans, so a full world's CPU is bounded: ≈20–30 % of a 0.25 vCPU Graviton task, while an idle task sits at ≈3 % [E10, E15]. With any sensible CPU target (40–50 %), the service would stay at one task while every world is full and new players wait. The scaling question is really "are there free seats?", so the primary policy tracks a custom metric, WorldOccupancyPct = humans / 4 × 100, averaged over all tasks, with a target of 75 %. Target tracking then keeps tasks ≈ ceil(humans / 3), about one empty world in four. CPU target tracking at 50 % stays as a safety net (for example if world size or NPC count were raised). Application Auto Scaling scales out when either policy asks and scales in only when both agree.

Scale-in must not end games in progress. Each task enables ECS task scale-in protection when its world starts (renewed every 5 minutes, expiring after 15 if the process dies) and releases it when the world empties. During the test, the four tasks hosting players were protected and the newly started empty task was not [E12]. The autoscaler can therefore only remove empty worlds.

Scale-out experiment

Fourteen synthetic players (load-tests/websocket-load.js) joined through CloudFront (wss://…/ws), ramping over 30 s, for 25 minutes. They behave like real clients: they join, retry ROOM_FULL, steer with wandering input at 10 Hz, boost occasionally, respawn after dying, and ping every 2 s. The load is genuine gameplay. Nothing is a busy loop. Because one world takes four humans, ten players are initially rejected, which is exactly the situation the occupancy policy exists for.

Each step needed three consecutive one-minute datapoints above target plus the 60 s scale-out cooldown, so the fleet grew by one world roughly every 2–3 minutes, and the sequence 1→2→3→4→5 followed ceil(tasks × occupancy / 75). New tasks were registered and healthy 30–45 s after each decision. Across the run the load tester recorded 0 connection errors, WebSocket RTT p50 16 ms / p95 ⟦RTT_P95⟧ ms from Brisbane via the BNE edge, and a snapshot interval of p50 67 ms (15 Hz) [E14]. Per-task CPU stayed at 20–30 % and the p99 tick time stayed under ⟦TICK_P99⟧ ms of the 33 ms budget [E15].

Load distribution. The ALB uses round robin for new connections. Combined with ROOM_FULL and client retry, this acts as simple decentralised matchmaking: the load tester's per-task counts show players spread evenly over the new worlds as they appeared (4/4/4/2) [E14], with targets healthy in all three AZs [E13].

One finding changed the understanding of graceful shutdown. Because ECS drains the ALB target before sending SIGTERM, the ALB, not the application, ends WebSocket sessions when the deregistration delay expires. The server's bye message is only delivered when the process itself initiates shutdown. Resilience therefore rests on the client's reconnect path, and the measurement shows that path is fast.

5.3 Sustainability

  • Capacity follows demand. Target tracking on occupancy adds worlds only when players need seats and removes idle ones afterwards (scale-in evidence §4.5). The occupancy target trades a small amount of idle capacity (25 %) for players not waiting.
  • Idle worlds do no work. When the last human leaves, the world object is discarded and the tick does nothing: an idle task runs at ≈3 % CPU versus ≈25 % active [E10, E15]. NPCs never simulate without players.
  • Right-sizing from measurement. The smallest Fargate size (0.25 vCPU, 0.5 GB) was kept because a full world used ≈20–30 % CPU and ≈25 MB of memory, with p99 tick time well inside the 33 ms budget.
  • Graviton (ARM64) for both Fargate and Lambda: AWS states better energy efficiency per unit of work than comparable x86, at 20 % lower price here.
  • Less data moved. Area-of-interest culling, delta encoding and compression cut bytes per player by ~85 % relative to the first version. Moving fewer bytes means less network energy as well as lower cost.
  • Edge caching. Fingerprinted assets are cached for a year in browsers and at the edge, and the leaderboard for 10 s, so repeat requests never reach S3, the ALB, Lambda or DynamoDB [E6].
  • Managed and serverless components (Fargate, Lambda, SQS, DynamoDB on-demand) consume nothing while idle, instead of always-on instances sized for peak.

6.2 What failed and what changed

  • The first NPC brain let NPCs die every ~15 s. Death-cause instrumentation (rather than guessing) found head-to-head collisions while chasing the same food, and a targeted fix raised median lifetime to 23 s.
  • A growth bug left the newest tail section undefined for one tick. It serialised as null and was drawn at (0, 0). It was found by checking rendered snake extents in the browser, fixed in grow(), and covered by a regression test.
  • The first load test gave up on ROOM_FULL after ~2 minutes, before auto-scaling could add capacity (~3 min of alarm datapoints + ~1 min of task start). The retry policy became "five fast jittered attempts, then bounded exponential backoff for ~3 minutes", in both the browser and the load tester.
  • Two players in the same browser share localStorage, hence the same player ID. An early "replace the older connection" rule would have made two tabs evict each other in a loop, so it was removed.

6.3 What I would do differently

  • Check account permissions before writing templates (a scripted probe of create/delete/tag actions), rather than discovering denies through failed stacks.
  • Add predictive or scheduled scaling for known peaks. Target tracking needs ~3 minutes of datapoints, so a sudden rush waits a few minutes for new worlds.
  • Use a binary snapshot format and a 10 Hz snapshot rate before reaching for compression, and measure the CPU/egress trade-off on Fargate itself.
  • Request a certificate (or use a VPC origin) so the CloudFront→ALB hop is encrypted, and use dedicated security groups once permitted.
